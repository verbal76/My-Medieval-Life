MY MEDIEVAL LIFE — BUILD 25 REVISION 1

OWNER DIRECTIVE: COMPLETE REGIONAL WORLD RECONSTRUCTION

Morris Geography, Living Settlements, Rural Hamlets, Roads, Rivers, Landmarks, and Population

OWNER AUTHORIZATION: APPROVED FOR BUILD 25 REVISION 1

I am authorizing the geographical reconstruction and expansion of the existing world.

I want the world to feel like a real, inhabited medieval region inspired by William Morris's The Well at the World's End, not six clusters of houses arranged along a narrow north–south strip.

Follow the novel's established geography as faithfully as practical. Where Morris leaves geographical details unspecified, creatively develop the world in a way that fits his setting.

This work belongs in Build 25 Revision 1, not a future expansion.

Do not stop existing workers, discard completed work, or restart the project. Integrate this directive into the current parallel development effort.

---

1. Source material is authoritative

Begin with the existing repository references:

- "docs/world/B_GAZETTEER.md"
- "docs/expansion18/SETTLEMENTS.md"
- "docs/world/H_WORLD_GENERATOR_SPEC.md"
- Original novel references already verified during the geographical audit.

Establish three classifications:

A. Canonical geography: Locations, relationships, distances, directions, and landmarks explicitly supported by Morris.

B. Reasonable interpretation: Details inferred from descriptions or travel times, clearly identified as interpretations.

C. Game-original expansion: New villages, hamlets, roads, businesses, and countryside details created for gameplay.

Preserve canonical facts. Do not present invented details as novel canon.

Where descriptions are ambiguous, choose a geographically plausible interpretation and document the reasoning.

The novel should guide the world, not unnecessarily restrict our ability to make it playable.

2. Eliminate the artificial settlement strip

The current map has six principal settlements arranged in a narrow corridor approximately 70 tiles wide.

I do not like this arrangement.

Reconstruct the regional layout to create a believable landscape with meaningful east–west exploration.

However, do not randomly scatter canonical settlements merely to break up the line.

If the novel establishes that the main journey proceeds southward, preserve that overall relationship.

The main road may still be one continuous route, but it should meander naturally through the landscape.

It should:

- Curve around hills, forests, marshes, cliffs, and waterways.
- Follow valleys and usable terrain.
- Climb and descend appropriate passes.
- Bend east and west where geography makes sense.
- Pass through settlements and important crossroads.
- Include historically plausible bridges, fords, and trails.
- Avoid ruler-straight connections between towns.

A road can travel generally south without being a straight vertical line.

Use the existing traffic, wear, paving, repair, and overgrowth systems.

Preserve the distinctions between maintained roads, dirt roads, ancient routes, and trails.

The surrounding countryside must have meaningful destinations in multiple directions.

3. Restore the missing Morris geography

Implement the important geographical features identified by the audit.

These include, where supported by the source:

- The Downs, with broad open chalk uplands.
- Bear Hill and its chalk figures.
- Bear Castle and its ancient earthworks.
- The old Greenway.
- The seven grave-mounds.
- Saint Ann's Chapel.
- The two additional small villages described near the Downs.
- The waste.
- The Wood Perilous.
- The cultivated valleys and vineyard country south of Higham.
- Other relevant documented landmarks and terrain features from the gazetteer.

These should be real world locations or geographical regions, not merely labels on the map.

The Downs must look and behave differently from ordinary forest countryside.

Respect the novel's description of open, exposed, relatively treeless and waterless uplands. Do not cover them with random forest or generate abundant streams through them.

The Wood Perilous should have a distinct identity appropriate to the source material.

Do not invent special supernatural mechanics merely because a place has an evocative name.

Use existing art and gameplay systems wherever possible.

4. Reconstruct the principal settlements

All six existing principal settlements must become physically explorable, functioning communities.

They should not be rows of identical houses.

Each community must reflect its size, economy, geography, population, and literary identity.

Your starting village / Upmeads country

Preserve its established playable systems while aligning the surrounding geography with the source.

Include the appropriate Upmeads Water relationship, crossing points, agricultural countryside, and regional road connections.

Wulstead — market town

Create a recognizable market town with appropriate streets, a market square or trading area, the documented fountain or market cross, businesses, homes, storage, and surrounding farmland.

Netherton — hamlet

Include the documented stream, church, canons' house, and appropriately modest residential and agricultural development.

Garton — village

Reflect its documented location and identity, including the street cross, barn, surrounding fields, and relationship to the Downs.

Higham-on-the-Way — abbey town

This must be a substantial, distinctive town rather than a handful of houses.

Include the documented town walls, white castle on its knoll, abbey, church towers, market activity, streets, residences, and appropriate economic infrastructure.

Its scale should reflect its importance in the novel.

Bourton Abbas — abbey village

Reflect the documented grey stone buildings, abbey-village character, surrounding valley, cultivated land, and vineyard country where geographically appropriate.

Use the gazetteer to correct or supplement these summaries.

Do not force every settlement into the same rectangular template.

Buildings should be placed along streets and around plausible civic and economic spaces.

5. Add small original hamlets throughout the region

NEW OWNER DECISION: APPROVED

I want additional small hamlets scattered naturally throughout the countryside, including east and west of the principal route.

These are game-original communities, not locations falsely attributed to Morris.

A typical new hamlet should contain approximately five or six families or households.

Most should have:

- Five or six occupied households.
- Appropriate homes and small outbuildings.
- Gardens, fields, livestock, or other local livelihoods.
- One or two small businesses or shops where economically sustainable.
- A local path or road connection.
- Access to water through streams, wells, or another valid existing source.

Not every hamlet needs identical facilities.

One might have a smith and a small provisions seller. Another might have a mill, carpenter, weaver, or farmer who sells surplus produce.

Businesses must exist because real residents operate them and real economic conditions support them.

Do not generate free inventory or infinite merchant money.

Placement

Distribute hamlets organically according to:

- Fertile land.
- Water availability.
- Road access.
- Nearby resources.
- Existing settlement spacing.
- Suitable terrain.
- Plausible local economic activity.

Some may sit near the main road, others along side roads, woodland clearings, valleys, or agricultural areas.

Avoid uniform spacing, straight rows, and obvious repeating patterns.

Do not fill intentionally uninhabited canonical wilderness with settlements.

The Downs, waste, and Wood Perilous must retain their documented character.

Number of hamlets

Do not arbitrarily impose a large fixed count.

Investigate the map's actual usable area, travel distances, economy, performance, and population budget.

Choose a modest, believable initial number that makes the countryside feel inhabited without turning it into continuous suburban development.

Record the chosen number and justification.

If the appropriate count is materially uncertain, present measured alternatives rather than guessing.

Names

Create names that sound appropriate to the world of William Morris.

Favor restrained medieval English naming conventions, including landscape, farming, woodland, stream, and local-history references.

Names should sound like natural places, not fantasy-game quest locations.

Check for collisions with existing canonical and game-original place names.

Document all newly invented names as game-original.

Give each hamlet a small but coherent identity based on its terrain and economy.

6. Every settlement must participate in the real simulation

This is non-negotiable.

Do not create decorative villages containing simulated ghosts.

Each household must correspond to actual persistent residents in the existing population system.

Residents must retain:

- Unique identities.
- Ages and relationships.
- Family and household membership.
- Occupations and skills.
- Money and possessions.
- Needs and survival behavior.
- Marriage, childbirth, aging, and mortality.
- Employment and trade relationships.
- Save/load continuity.

Shops must correspond to actual businesses.

Homes must correspond to actual households.

Fields, workshops, markets, and other economic structures must correspond to real gameplay systems where those systems exist.

Visitors and travelers must be able to interact with these communities through the existing travel and economy architecture.

A hamlet should not receive a fresh batch of NPCs whenever the player arrives.

Use the existing simulation and persistence systems, not a separate simplified hamlet simulation.

7. Population and economic balance

Adding hamlets adds households and potentially increases the regional population.

Investigate this impact before implementation.

Do not silently duplicate existing residents or generate artificial wealth.

Determine whether new communities begin with a properly accounted-for initial population, whether existing populations are redistributed, or whether some hamlets develop organically.

Document the initial-world population model separately from subsequent births, migration, and household formation.

Ensure the economy can sustain the added communities.

Verify:

- Food production and consumption.
- Local trade and merchant inventory.
- Money conservation.
- Goods conservation.
- Employment and wages.
- Construction resources.
- Livestock and agricultural output.
- Migration and travel demand.
- Demographic stability over long simulations.

The world should not become economically unstable simply because we added picturesque hamlets.

9. Roads and transportation

The region should contain a believable hierarchy of routes.

The principal Morris journey remains geographically recognizable.

Beyond it, add appropriate:

- Roads connecting principal settlements.
- Side roads to rural hamlets.
- Farm tracks.
- Woodland trails.
- Historic routes.
- Bridges and fords.
- Paths connecting wilderness homesteads where appropriate.

Do not create an unrealistic perfect road grid.

Road quality and construction should reflect settlement importance, available materials, traffic, and existing world rules.

Travelers must navigate the actual roads and terrain.

Routefinding should consider crossings, terrain, and road conditions.

No teleporting between settlements merely because they have economic relationships.

I want to walk out of my village and feel as though I'm entering a real medieval world.
