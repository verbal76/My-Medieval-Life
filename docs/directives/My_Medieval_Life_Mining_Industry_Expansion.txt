MY MEDIEVAL LIFE

Mining & Industrial Development Expansion
Owner-approved design specification | Proposed dedicated Build 17 or 18 |
19 October 2026
DESIGN STATUS: Approved direction and scope. Exact mechanics, costs, balancing values,
map technology and build number remain to be designed and validated.



1. Vision and purpose
This is not simply a mining update. It is a village-wide industrial development expansion.
Mining drives demand for carpenters, forges, haulers, builders, quarry workers and new
specialists. As settlements acquire skills, tools, infrastructure and institutional knowledge,
villages can develop into towns and eventually major settlements. The player is not
required for this progression: capable NPC communities should pursue it autonomously
when circumstances support it.

Core principle: a particular mineral deposit is finite, but the mining industry can continue
through prospecting, investment, excavation, transport, trade and specialization. Never
create ore or wealth from nothing merely to preserve the economy.



2. Five-stage mining development pathway
Stage 1 — Surface deposits
The existing mineral-bearing rock is the visible top deposit. Colored ore indicators mark
extractable mineral. When the colored indicators are depleted, the remaining gray rock
can still be mined for ordinary stone. Depleted ore does not automatically replenish. Stone
is moved into a quarry/stone-handling production chain.

Stage 2 — Prospecting and assay
Prospectors interpret subtle natural clues, investigate nearby terrain with specialized
equipment, collect samples and have findings evaluated by an assayer or equivalent
specialist. A clue indicates a search area, not a guaranteed deposit or its exact location.
Newly located deposits remain finite.

Stage 3 — Open-pit / strip mining
Workers remove topsoil, dirt and rock to excavate a roughly square pit with terraced sides
and a descending spiral ramp or switchback access. Deeper strata may reveal new finite
deposits. Excavation creates real spoil, access, hauling and safety requirements.

Stage 4 — Underground mines and caves
Suitable rock faces and underground formations may support shafts, galleries, chambers
and enterable caves. In isometric view, roofs/overhead terrain hide or become
transparent so the player can see characters inside. Supports, lighting, ventilation, access,
navigation and hauling are genuine dependencies.

Stage 5 — Mature mining and industrial networks
Advanced settlements develop dedicated quarry and mining operations, minecarts, track
laying, track fabrication, cart assembly, repair, hauling logistics, storage, assay services
and trade links. Expansion is supported by an actual workforce and resource economy
rather than a simple unlock button.



3. Settlement capability levels and growth
Mining capability is a property of the settlement as a functioning community, not just the
skill of one NPC. A village can learn to prospect, build specialized infrastructure and train
workers; that capability enables greater economic complexity and contributes to its
evolution into a town or major settlement.

requires real prerequisites and can regress if workers, equipment or knowledge
disappear.

4. Prospecting: reading the land
Geological hints should be natural and discoverable: faintly stained or discolored soil,
mineral flecks, exposed veins, unusual rock fragments or changes in nearby outcrops. The
owner specifically proposes a ground stain of a different color as an initial example. These
are hints, not an X marking a guaranteed deposit.

   The terrain generator places finite deposits and plausible surface evidence in
    proximity, with uncertainty and occasional misleading clues.
   Prospectors examine terrain, choose search locations, use a prospecting toolkit, collect
    physical samples and carry them to an assayer.
   An assay can estimate mineral type and quality with uncertainty; better tools and
    experience improve reliability, not omniscience.
   Discoveries are recorded and become actionable knowledge only for people or
    institutions that receive the information.
   The future large map must provide further geological opportunity without assuming
    the current map is already large.



5. New careers and the industrial supply chain
Specialist professions
   Prospector: surveys terrain, recognizes clues, samples, marks probable sites and
    reports discoveries.
   Assayer: tests samples and evaluates mineral composition or quality using tools, fuel
    and materials as appropriate.
   Miner and excavation crew: extract surface ore, stone, earth and deeper deposits.
   Quarry worker / stonecutter: extracts, sorts and prepares usable stone.
   Mine engineer or foreman (proposed role): plans safe ramps, shafts, supports and
    excavation work.
   Track layer: installs and maintains mine tracks and associated earthworks.
   Minecart builder / repairer: assembles carts from actual timber and forged
    components.
   Haulers, carpenters, blacksmiths and builders: supply and maintain every stage of the
    operation.

Equipment, workshops and recipes
A prospecting toolkit should be forge-produced, drawing on existing crafting rules and
physical inventories. New equipment may include picks, hammers, sample bags, sieves,
assay instruments, timber supports, shovels, wheelbarrows, minecarts, axles, wheels, rail
sections, track fasteners, lamps and repair parts. These are candidate recipes, not final
approved quantities or metallurgy.

For each item, Claude must define realistic inputs, manufacturing workstation, labor,
wear, repair, storage, transport and consumers. Do not allow one new tool to bypass
existing carpentry, forging or hauling systems.

6. Stone, spoil and dirt are real resources
After surface ore is exhausted, the gray host rock is still useful stone. Quarrying must
integrate with the existing construction economy, including roads, foundations, walls and
masonry where supported. Excavated dirt and topsoil are separately tracked materials
rather than deleted terrain.

Candidate uses for earth include grading building plots, road embankments, backfilling,
defensive earthworks and landscaping. Agricultural use must distinguish suitable topsoil
from poor subsoil. Storage and disposal have labor and space costs. All uses require
recipes and world-system integration before implementation.



7. Mine geometry, caves and presentation
Open pits should visibly deepen through excavation, with terraced walls and a navigable
spiral or switchback ramp around the perimeter. Underground workings should be
enterable, not merely decorative. In the isometric presentation, overhead terrain becomes
transparent, hidden or cut away when needed to keep player and NPCs visible.

Before promising terrain excavation, inspect whether the existing map, collision and
navigation systems support layered elevations, altered ground, interior spaces and
persistence. Hidden animal breeding refuges do not by themselves establish enterable
cave support. Assess pathfinding, rendering, camera, lighting, stability, save migration and
eventual multiplayer replication.



8. Autonomous settlement development
NPC settlements should evaluate resource scarcity, local geology, workforce, tools,
available funds, trade opportunities and construction demand. A village with sufficient
capability may train a prospector, commission equipment, investigate clues, open a
quarry or expand a mine without a player issuing orders. A failed prospecting venture
costs time and resources; a successful one can create new jobs and attract families,
merchants and craftspeople.

Settlement advancement must emerge from demonstrated capability and sustained
institutions. Track mining technology/capability, industrial workforce, infrastructure and
productive capacity independently of population and political status. Skills may be
retained through apprenticeships, records and living practitioners; loss of all expertise
can constrain future development.



9. Economy, scarcity and balancing safeguards
   Ore bodies and stone layers remain finite. No automatic replenishment of depleted
    veins.
   Prospecting does not guarantee success; expansion needs time, labor, materials and
    investment.

10. Release and implementation boundaries
Reserve this as a dedicated industry-development expansion, tentatively Build 17 or 18.
The exact number is not locked. Do not derail or silently incorporate the full expansion
into the Build 15/16 equilibrium work. Capture requirements now, conduct architecture
review, and plan staged development on the normal remote GitHub staging workflow.
Keep current authorized release/test gates intact.

Suggested implementation phases within the dedicated build
1. A. Audit terrain, mining, inventory, recipes, AI, pathfinding, saves and map
    generation; establish architecture and measurable acceptance tests.
2. B. Preserve exhausted ore outcrops as quarryable stone; add quarry chain and stone
    logistics.
3. C. Add natural prospecting clues, specialist profession, forge-produced toolkit, samples
    and assay workflow.
4. D. Add new finite deposit discovery and settlement capability levels with autonomous
    NPC adoption.
5. E. Add open-pit excavation, spoil handling, ramps, hauling and geological layers where
    architecture supports them.
6. F. Add underground/cave mining, cutaway presentation, minecarts, track construction
    and full industrial dependencies.
7. G. Run 100-/365-day multi-seed simulations and gameplay tests, validate saves,
    economic conservation and no resource spawning.



11. Acceptance criteria
   A depleted colored ore deposit remains mineable for ordinary stone without
    regenerating ore.
   At least one prospecting clue leads to a search area, not a guaranteed exact ore
    location; failures are possible.
   Prospectors and assayers use actual produced equipment and consume actual time
    and resources.

   Open-pit excavation, if implemented, removes and accounts for dirt/stone and exposes
