FINAL OWNER DECISIONS: BUSINESS OWNERSHIP, EMPLOYMENT, AND ECONOMIC EXPANSION

DECISION 1 — SEPARATE BUSINESS AND HOUSEHOLD FINANCES

Approved: Business finances must remain separate from household finances.

This was previously established under ECON-P3 and must also apply to newly founded workshops and other operating businesses.

Requirements:

- Each business maintains its own financial accounts.
- Business revenue belongs to the business.
- Business expenses, materials, wages, and operating costs are paid from business funds.
- Owners may invest personal money into their businesses.
- Owners may withdraw profits according to the existing economic rules.
- A struggling business cannot automatically consume the household's protected living reserves.
- Business failure must have genuine economic consequences.
- No money may be created, destroyed, or duplicated through transfers.

Reuse and extend "BusinessMoney", existing till records, and the established transaction system.

Preserve prior owner-approved rules for household necessities, business reserves, and transfers.

Do not create a competing accounting system.

DECISION 2 — EMPLOYEES MUST BE PAID

Approved: Genuine employees receive compensation for their work.

The current workshop arrangement permits independent craftspeople to use another owner's stations while retaining their own production and earnings.

That arrangement may continue, but it is NOT employment.

There must be a clear distinction between:

Independent craftsperson: Uses workshop facilities with permission, produces their own goods, and retains their earnings under the applicable agreement.

Business employee: Works for an employer under agreed terms, receives wages, and produces goods or services for the employer.

Requirements:

- Businesses can hire workers.
- Employees receive agreed compensation.
- Employers own the production performed under the employment agreement.
- Materials and operating costs are accounted for.
- Employee wages are genuine economic transfers.
- Hiring decisions reflect qualifications, availability, business needs, affordability, and NPC judgment.
- Businesses cannot sustain employees indefinitely without the money to pay them.
- Employees may leave employment under the existing social and economic systems.

Apply these principles equally to player-owned and NPC-owned businesses.

Use existing labor-market and contract mechanics wherever appropriate.

DECISION 3 — UNFINISHED BUSINESS CONSTRUCTION

Approved: No automatic financial refunds for abandoned business construction.

However, households and players must never become permanently trapped by a building project they cannot complete.

Requirements:

- An unfinished business can be voluntarily canceled or abandoned.
- Construction may also be abandoned when the household's circumstances make completion unreasonable.
- Materials already purchased are not magically refunded as money.
- Existing recoverable-material rules should apply.
- Completed work and consumed resources remain genuine economic costs.
- Property ownership and land transactions remain consistent.
- Abandoning a workshop must release the household from its active construction obligation.
- The household can pursue other needs, including housing and survival.
- Construction cancellation must not duplicate or destroy resources.

Reuse the existing construction cancellation system rather than inventing a new one.

DECISION 4 — PERMANENT EMPLOYMENT IS NOT RESTRICTED TO MINERS

Approved: Permanent employment must be available across appropriate professions.

There is no gameplay reason that only miners should have access to permanent employment.

The existing miner implementation is a starting point, not the intended final employment model.

Extend permanent employment into a general economic system.

Appropriate roles include existing occupations such as:

- Miners and prospectors.
- Woodcutters and other resource gatherers.
- Farmers and agricultural workers.
- Blacksmiths and other skilled craftspeople.
- Carpenters.
- Tanners.
- Cooks and food producers.
- Shopkeepers and attendants.
- Construction workers.
- Other professions already supported by the game's actual production and service mechanics.

Do not invent production capabilities for occupations whose underlying work systems do not exist.

Instead, integrate the employment framework with the professions and workplaces already implemented.

Employment requirements

1. Both players and NPC business owners may employ workers.
2. Employment persists across days until legitimately terminated.
3. Employees must agree to the terms.
4. Wages should use existing negotiation and labor-market systems where available.
5. The employer must be able to afford the employee.
6. Workers perform their actual professional tasks using their existing skills and mechanics.
7. Goods produced under employment belong to the employer.
8. Workers receive their agreed compensation.
9. Employers may dismiss employees.
10. Employees may resign or change jobs under appropriate circumstances.
11. Employment cannot silently interfere with existing obligations.
12. Employment survives saving and loading.
13. Multiplayer employment actions must follow the existing host-authoritative validation rules.

Avoid creating a separate permanent-employment implementation for every profession.

Establish a shared employment framework with profession-specific work behavior.

The distinction is simple: A profession determines what work someone performs. Employment determines whom they work for and how they are compensated.

The current 10-coin miner wage is provisional. Review wages within the existing economy rather than applying that amount universally.

DECISION 5 — BUSINESSES MAY BE BUILT, PURCHASED, SOLD, AND EXPANDED

Approved: Players and NPCs must have multiple legitimate ways to obtain or dispose of businesses.

A person should not be forced to construct a new business when an existing business is available for purchase.

A. Building businesses

Preserve the existing construction options.

The repository already supports:

- Workshops attached to residential properties.
- Standalone workshops on separately claimed land.
- Multiple workshop types.
- Business counters, storefronts, and market operations.

These must remain available.

A person who already owns a home may add an appropriate workshop to that property.

A person with sufficient resources may also establish a standalone workshop elsewhere on legally acquired land.

The distinction between a home workshop and a standalone business must remain meaningful.

Both options must follow existing property, construction, resource, and ownership rules.

B. Buying businesses

Allow players and NPCs to purchase available existing businesses from willing owners.

Purchases must involve:

- A legitimate seller.
- A legitimate buyer.
- An agreed price.
- Actual available funds.
- Transfer of the agreed business assets.
- Correct ownership changes.
- Appropriate treatment of inventory, equipment, employees, outstanding obligations, and operating funds.
- Existing property rights and permissions.

The seller must receive the payment.

No money or property may be duplicated.

Business purchases should be evaluated through existing NPC economic judgment and negotiation systems.

C. Selling businesses

Allow players and NPCs to offer businesses for sale.

Example:

A villager owns a blacksmith workshop but wants to become a carpenter.

They may offer the smithy for sale.

Another villager who wants to operate a smithy may purchase it.

The original owner receives the agreed payment and can use those resources to establish or purchase a carpentry business.

A change in occupation should not require an owner to abandon valuable business property without compensation.

D. Home-attached workshops

Pay particular attention to workshops built as additions to residential properties.

Selling a workshop must not automatically sell the family's home.

Inspect the existing ownership representation.

Where the workshop and home cannot currently be transferred independently, design the smallest safe extension that distinguishes business ownership or operating rights from residential property ownership.

Any use of another owner's land or attached premises must be supported by a legitimate agreement and appropriate permission.

Do not silently transfer residential land, housing, or household possessions as part of a business sale.

E. Standalone businesses and expansion

A player or villager should be able to progress from a small home workshop to a larger standalone enterprise.

Business expansion may involve acquiring additional premises, equipment, facilities, and employees through the existing economic systems.

Multiple businesses may be owned where legitimate property, financial, staffing, and operating requirements permit it.

F. Investigate the apparent two-shop restriction

In an earlier playtest, I appeared to be limited to two shops.

Before modifying limits, investigate whether this was caused by:

- An actual coded ownership limit.
- The number of initially available properties.
- Building-placement restrictions.
- Property or licensing requirements.
- A user-interface limitation.
- A limitation that existed in the older build but has since been corrected.

Document the actual finding.

Do not invent an artificial two-shop maximum.

Business ownership should be limited by realistic in-game constraints, not an arbitrary count unless an existing owner-approved rule explicitly requires one.

PROFESSIONAL MOBILITY

The complete system must support career changes.

A blacksmith should be able to become a carpenter.

A carpenter should be able to become a blacksmith.

A worker should be able to become a business owner.

A business owner should be able to sell their enterprise and pursue another profession.

These transitions must use the game's existing skill progression, experience, career ambitions, property, and labor-market rules.

Business ownership alone should not magically grant professional skill.

NPCs should make these decisions organically according to their aspirations, financial circumstances, skills, opportunities, and judgment.
