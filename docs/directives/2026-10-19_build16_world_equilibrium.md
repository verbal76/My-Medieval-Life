# Owner directive, 2026-10-19: Build 16 world equilibrium (verbatim)

MY MEDIEVAL LIFE — BUILD 16 WORLD EQUILIBRIUM DIRECTIVE

OWNER'S FUNDAMENTAL DESIGN REQUIREMENT

By Build 16, I expect My Medieval Life to have a mostly self-sustaining, interconnected world where human civilization and the natural ecosystem can coexist, compete, adapt, recover, and generally progress into the future.

The world must not require the player to survive.

Losses are expected. Individual NPCs die, families disappear, animals are hunted, businesses fail, crops are lost, and communities experience hardship.

But the world as a whole should generally remain viable and capable of progressing across generations.

The player is the infection introduced into that system.

This means the player is an external disruptive influence who can destabilize, exploit, protect, improve, or fundamentally transform an otherwise autonomous world.

1. Human civilization must sustain itself

Without player intervention, NPCs must be capable of:

- Producing and distributing food.
- Forming families and raising new generations.
- Maintaining a viable population over time.
- Building, repairing, and replacing housing.
- Operating businesses and professions.
- Acquiring finite resources.
- Responding to shortages.
- Creating and spending money through legitimate economic systems.
- Maintaining trade relationships.
- Recovering from disasters and individual losses.
- Filling vacant jobs and civic positions.
- Adapting when businesses fail or important NPCs die.

The population does not need to grow continuously, but it must not inevitably collapse because essential systems are missing.

2. Nature must sustain itself

Wildlife populations must have believable mechanisms for:

- Reproduction.
- Aging and natural death.
- Predation.
- Competition.
- Migration and dispersal.
- Habitat selection.
- Local extinction.
- Gradual recolonization.
- Recovery from overhunting.

Preserve the approved protected breeding-refuge concept.

Predators must not inevitably eliminate all prey, and hunting must not inevitably eliminate every species across the entire world.

Population fluctuations and local extinctions are acceptable. Unavoidable global extinction caused by incomplete simulation mechanics is not.

Do not introduce unlimited instant respawning to conceal ecological failures.

3. Humans and nature must interact

Human activity must affect the ecosystem.

Examples include:

- Hunting reduces animal populations.
- Farming changes available habitat.
- Predators threaten livestock.
- Deforestation affects resources and wildlife.
- Overharvesting creates shortages.
- Population growth increases food and construction demand.
- Resource scarcity changes professions, trade, and settlement development.

Nature must also affect civilization.

Poor harvests, predator pressure, depleted resources, and changing wildlife populations should influence real NPC decisions.

Neither system should exist independently as a disconnected background simulation.

4. Humans must maintain a functioning society

NPCs must cooperate, compete, remember, trade, disagree, and adapt.

A successful village should not depend on every individual NPC surviving.

When a carpenter dies, another NPC should eventually be capable of entering that profession.

When a household loses its provider, surviving members should respond.

When leadership changes, government should continue.

When a business fails, its economic role should be capable of being filled by another business or through trade.

Relationships, generational memory, politics, inheritance, crime, and reputation must create meaningful consequences without permanently preventing society from functioning.

5. The player is the disruptive variable

The world must be simulated independently of player participation.

Then introduce the player and measure the consequences.

A player might:

- Overhunt an area.
- Corner a market.
- Destroy a business.
- Build a successful trading enterprise.
- Rescue a struggling family.
- Murder a political leader.
- Become mayor.
- Create a criminal organization.
- Protect wildlife.
- Introduce new production capacity.
- Cause shortages or relieve them.
- Change the future of an entire settlement.

The world must react through its actual interconnected systems.

Do not secretly protect the world from legitimate player consequences.

If the player destroys the local economy, that destruction should be possible.

If the player restores it, recovery should also be possible.

The distinction is that an untouched world should generally be viable, while a player can meaningfully alter its trajectory.

6. Build 16 acceptance testing

Establish reproducible simulations with the player completely inactive.

Test multiple seeds and starting conditions over:

- 100 game days.
- 365 game days.
- Multiple simulated generations, where supported.

Measure:

- Human population and replacement rates.
- Births and deaths.
- Household formation.
- Food availability.
- Housing construction and maintenance.
- Employment and profession continuity.
- Wealth and economic circulation.
- Trade.
- Resource depletion and adaptation.
- Wildlife populations by species.
- Predator-prey relationships.
- Local extinction and recolonization.
- Settlement growth and decline.
- Government and civic continuity.
- Recovery after ordinary disasters.

Do not require monotonically increasing populations or wealth.

Instead, demonstrate that the world has functioning feedback mechanisms and does not consistently spiral into extinction, starvation, permanent unemployment, or economic paralysis.

Then run separate player-intervention scenarios to verify that meaningful disruption changes the results.

Use multiple seeds so one unusually successful simulation cannot conceal a systemic problem.

7. Investigate the known failures

The previous 365-day simulation reported:

- Human population declining from 100 to 69.
- Wildlife reaching zero.
- No new houses being constructed.

Treat these as important diagnostic findings.

Determine why each occurred.

A population decline alone does not establish failure, but combined with wildlife extinction and no construction, these results raise serious concerns about long-term world viability.

Correct underlying causes rather than introducing artificial population or resource injections that conceal broken mechanics.

8. Build progression

Build 14 remains the near-term playable checkpoint.

Build 15 should implement foundational improvements and address the most important simulation failures.

Build 16 is the target for demonstrating a broadly self-sustaining, interconnected world.

Do not delay Build 14 for this entire objective.

Do not close this directive merely because a short simulation passes.

Record the objective in the permanent requirements register, connect it to the existing World Design Rules and Interlocking Systems Manifest, and carry it through Builds 14, 15, and 16.

Document the results in the in-game Wiki only as features actually become implemented.

FINAL DESIGN STANDARD

I want to be able to start a new world, leave the player completely alone, and watch that world continue living.

Families should rise and fall.

Businesses should open and close.

Animals should reproduce, migrate, hunt, and die.

Villages should struggle, adapt, and sometimes prosper.

New generations should inherit a world shaped by the generations before them.

The world should experience losses without inevitably dying.

By Build 16, the world itself should be the functioning simulation. The player should be the variable that changes its history, not the mechanism keeping it alive.
