Chapel economy correction: before and after a year

Giving to the chapel is now each villager's own weekly choice, and the chapel gives what it does not need to the poorest households. Over a simulated year (two normal runs (seeds 1 and 2) and one with the Living Economy on (seed 1)), the chapel's share of the village's coins fell from 76.7–83.4% to 0.9–1.2%, and median household cash rose from 4–11 to 161–203 coins. Coins and items were conserved throughout.

1. What the code did before

  • Giving was automatic. Every seventh day, every adult whose fixed piety was 0.3 or more lost a share of their spare coins, and households a share of their wheat. Nobody chose; there was no attendance and no difference between refusing and being unable.
  • No penalties existed. Nothing in judgment or reputation touched the chapel, so nothing had to be removed.
  • The reserve was fixed at a week of clergy wages plus wages owed.
  • Alms almost never paid. They were capped at a household's shortfall in essentials, and food in the larder cancels that shortfall. A household with grain but no coins got nothing, so the fund held 77–83% of all coins by the end of the year.

2. What changed

  • A choice, every week. Each adult and elder who can spare a coin decides through the villagers' own decision system (the same one used for trade, work and courtship). It weighs their piety, what they can spare, the week's circumstances, the dependants their household feeds and their opinion of the preacher. Piety changes the odds and never decides: the devout sometimes keep their coins, those of little faith sometimes give.
  • Affordable, varied gifts. Only coins above what their household needs for the next days and a little savings are ever given; the amount varies. Someone with nothing to spare is not asked and is counted as unable, not as refusing. Declining changes nobody's opinion or reputation. Households decide the same way about spare wheat.
  • A modest reserve. The chapel keeps 14 days of clergy wages, wages owed, and what it has recently spent on relief food and seed.
  • Alms by hardship. Everything above the reserve goes to the poorest households, judged by coins, shop till, and food and firewood held, against two weeks of essentials at today's prices. A full larder no longer rules a household out; a household holding six weeks of essentials or more gets nothing, even when short of coins. Poorest per mouth first, each up to what it lacks.
  • Instrumentation records attendance, those unable, decisions, donors and refusals by wealth bracket, gift sizes, wages, reserve, alms, recipients and their cash before and after, and household cash percentiles. It changes no behaviour.

The numbers (gift share 10%, 14-day reserve, two-week essentials line, six-week cutoff, decision weights) are my provisional proposals. The first values tried (25% gifts, one-week line) were measured over 30 days and rejected: the fund reached 34% of all coins within a month.

4. Where the coins are

Before: coins drain into the chapel and stay there. After: the villagers' purses hold most of the money all year. On seed 2 the fund rose to about a tenth of all coins between days 90 and 260 and then fell back to its reserve. Few households qualified for alms in those months, so the surplus waited. The rule held it back rather than handing out money with no hardship to meet.

With money in purses the market roughly doubles: twice the bids, negotiations, deals and contracts. Jobs rise from 46 to 63 and nights without firewood fall from 4,532 to 790. The count of bids a household could not afford did not fall. Those are wishes priced above what the household can spare, and they rise with the number of people looking. The market still prices some goods beyond what households can pay. The chapel was not the only reason for this, and it needs its own look.
